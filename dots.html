Class Overview

In Design of Thermal Systems, I developed expertise in analyzing and constructing energy and thermo-fluid systems. I applied thermodynamic principles to complex cycles and did projects using analysis of systems with phase change, heat exchangers, and environmental requirements.

Design Homework Overview

Design Homeworks were individual projects that stretched over the course of the semester. There were three (3) in total, and each covered a different cycle or aspect of thermal systems. The first homework focused on the Rankine Cycle, with lots of research required to fill in open ended parameters and take into account environmental requirements. The second homework focused on the Brayton Cycle. Finally, the third coupled the both previous homeworks using heat exchanger analysis.

The goal of this third assignment is to combine the optimized Brayton and Rankine cycles while still maintaining the highest possible efficiency. Slight modifications might be required to ensure the optimal cycles are still physically realistic. Additionally, this assignment focuses on the coupling of the two cycles by designing a heat exchanger. The heat exchanger consists of a preheater, an evaporator, and a superheater, in that order.

Finally, we must consider the environmental implications and constraints when designing this combined cycle.

For the Rankine Cycle, the water is being pumped through the Potomac River. Applying the appropriate assumptions to the condenser will ensure that the environment is not being severely altered or harmed using this cycle. Ultimately, this system could be used underwater, so the temperature change from the inlet to outlet water cannot be very large. Lots of wildlife are impacted by even the smallest changes in temperature, so it is necessary to adhere to the regulations set in place by governing organizations. For the Brayton Cycle, the environment is a very important consideration. Ships burn a lot of fuel and that has a large impact on the environment. More eco-friendly fuels are being tested, but the cost has the potential to remain high until more research is done, or the fuels become more widely available. The biggest impact with combustion is all the carbon being put into the atmosphere.

Final Project Overview

The final project followed the same overall structure as the design homeworks, but all at once. My group decided to focus on a cooling system for an ice rink. This required the same cycle and heat exchanger analysis as before. Finally, we presented our findings at a poster showcase at the end of the year.

The primary objective of this lab report was to conduct an exploratory analysis of the thermal systems present in an Olympic sized ice rink and optimize the parameters in pursuit of the best coefficient of performance while varying the mass flow rate of coolant fluid. The working fluids selected for this task of optimization were ammonia NH3 as the primary working fluid and carbon dioxide CO2 as the secondary working fluid. FEAR discovered that by varying the mass flow rate of CO2 between 2 L/min and 100 L/min increased the heat exchanged between the two refrigeration cycles. The highest rate of heat transfer was found to be 0.7561 kW at a mass flow rate of 0.0030 kg/s, necessitating a chiller tubing length of 50.6098 m. Surprisingly, no strong correlation appeared between varying the the mass flow rate of the coolant fluid and the system’s coefficient of performance, which stayed constant at 6.6896. This indicates that other cycle parameters have more significant influence over the coefficient of performance, which will require further exploratory analysis to determine a procedure to optimize. Whether or not the mass flow rate of the cooling fluid needs to be increased depends on the desired heat transfer as well as pipe and cycle component limitations. These findings provide a foundation for future research aimed at refining the optimization of ice rink cooling systems, ultimately contributing to the development of more efficient and sustainable thermal solutions.
